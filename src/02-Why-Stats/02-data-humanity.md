## 1. Question about "Seven intersectional feminist principles ..."

### 1.1 Data and power

**Question**: According to D'Ignazio and Klein, what principles concern data and "power"? Define and discuss each principle below. Be sure to note the important questions to ask about data and power.

YOUR_RESPONSE_HERE

Here’s how I understand D’Ignazio & Klein’s take on data and power—and the key questions I should keep asking as I work with data. 

**Examine power**
Power shows up in who designs systems, what gets measured, and whose lives are made visible or invisible. So I should always ask: data science for whom? by whom? and with whose goals in mind? Those questions help me see whether a dataset or dashboard is reinforcing privilege or challenging it. 

**Challenge power**
It’s not enough to point out inequity; we should act. That can look like collecting “counterdata” when institutions ignore certain groups, or using analysis to hold agencies and companies accountable. The question here is: what concrete step would rebalance who benefits and who decides? 

**Elevate emotion and embodiment**
Numbers come from people with bodies, families, and grief. Treating emotion as valid knowledge keeps us from flattening harm into a statistic. I ask: whose lived experience needs to be part of how these numbers are interpreted and communicated? 

**Rethink binaries and hierarchies**
Rigid categories like male/female; counted/not counted often hide hierarchies. When categories erase people, the analysis bakes in bias. I ask: which categories are oversimplified, and who gets misclassified or excluded because of them? 

**Embrace pluralism**
Better knowledge comes from multiple perspectives—especially local, Indigenous, and experiential knowledge. I ask: whose voices and methods are missing from this project, and how do I bring them in (not just as “input” but as partners)? 

**Consider context**
Data aren’t neutral. Collection methods, testing access, reporting incentives—all shape the numbers. I ask: what social and historical conditions produced this data, and what comparisons are unsafe to make because contexts differ? 

**Make labor visible**
Data work is collective work—collection, cleaning, annotation, care, and community organizing. When that labor is invisible, power and credit concentrate at the top. I ask: whose labor made this possible, and how am I naming and valuing it? 

Overall, the authors' core idea is that data work is always power work. If I keep asking who benefits, who is harmed, who is missing, and who decides, I’m more likely to use data in ways that shift power toward equity rather than reinforce the status quo.

### 1.2 Data: Objective mirror, or not?

**Question**: What do D'Ignazio and Klein mean by issues of "false binaries and hidden hierarchies" (p. 3)? Then, explain how principles 3, 5, 6 and 7 each approach such issues.

False binaries and hidden hierarchies means we force people or ideas into two tidy boxes, like “man” and “woman” or “objective” and “subjective,” even when real life is more complicated. These splits do not just simplify things. They often hide a ranking where one side is treated as better than the other. D’Ignazio and Klein say this shows up in how we count, label, and decide what is worth tracking.

Principle 3, Elevate emotion and embodiment, questions the idea that data should be “neutral and rational” and that emotion is not valid. They say knowledge is “situated,” which means it comes from real people in real contexts. When we value lived experience, we notice what the simple split leaves out. Bringing bodies and feelings back into the picture shows how our culture often treats “cold facts” as more important than human experience.

Principle 5, Embrace pluralism, resists the habit of putting one expert view above all others. It says our understanding improves when we bring many perspectives together, especially local, indigenous, and experiential knowledge. When we do this, we stop one worldview from setting all the rules, and we reduce the power imbalance in one size fits all models.

Principle 6, Consider context, pushes back on the split between “objective data” and “messy politics.” They argue that data come from social conditions that are not equal. So context belongs in the analysis. This helps us reframe labels like “race as a risk factor” and point instead to racism and other structural forces, so we do not treat categories as natural or neutral.

Principle 7, Make labor visible, challenges the ranking of whose work matters. Data projects depend on many kinds of work, including collecting, cleaning, moderating, and care work. Much of this effort stays unseen. Naming and valuing it spreads credit and power more fairly, instead of letting it pool with a few people at the top.

### 2.2 Guyan's exigence for the book

**Question**: Why did Guyan write this book? What was his exigence?

**Response**: Guyan wrote this book because he kept running into confusion about LGBTQ data in real workplaces, especially in universities. People asked simple but important questions about tiny sample sizes, why to collect more than gender or race, and whether it was okay to ask about sexual orientation at all. He wanted to clear this up and give a practical guide that helps people use data to improve lives. He also saw groups using numbers about gender, sex, and sexuality to spread fear and push harmful ideas about LGBTQ people. So the book bridges queer theory and everyday data work, and it teaches readers to ask better questions about why we collect data, who it serves, and what action it should lead to.

### 2.3 "Prescriptive" versus "descriptive"

**Question**: What does Guyan mean when he labels terms like gender, sex, and sexuality as being descriptive—*not* prescriptive (see pp. 13-14 in particular)? Explain the differences between the two with an example from each of the terms.

**Response**: Guyan says these terms should describe people as they are, not tell people what they must be. He writes that the terms are “descriptive rather than prescriptive,” and that how someone defines gender, sex, or sexuality can differ by person, can be fluid, and can change over time. He also says these concepts depend on each other for meaning. 

For gender, a descriptive approach lets people say man, woman, something between, or beyond, and notes that expression and documents may not match. Some people may not identify with any gender. A prescriptive approach would force only male or female and even treat any change over time as an error. 

For sex, a descriptive approach includes bodies and lived realities and makes space for female, male, and also intersex and non-binary people. It also recognizes that sex and gender shape each other. A prescriptive approach treats sex as only biology and ignores people who do not fit a strict female or male box. 

Sexuality, in a descriptive sense, is how someone describes who they are attracted to or not, and the kinds of relationships they want. It can mean attraction to the same gender, a different gender, more than one gender, or no one at all. A prescriptive approach tries to force a single box like “straight” or “gay” and ignores other options or changes over time. If I say I am attracted to men and women, a descriptive approach lets me choose the words that fit me, like bisexual or pansexual, and change them later if I need to. A prescriptive approach would make me pick one narrow label and treat any change as a mistake.

### 2.4 "'Visibility is a trap'"

What does this expression mean, and why does Guyan draw parallels between Foucault's original use of it with issues of power and data? Be sure to illuminate your discussion with specific examples that Guyan uses in the book.

**Response**: “Visibility is a trap” means that being seen can also mean being watched and controlled. Foucault explains this with a prison example: when people think they might be watched, they change how they act. Guyan uses this idea to say that making LGBTQ people more visible in data can help, but it can also create new risks. On page 30, he shows this with Scotland. There is more LGBTQ data now, but life has not improved for everyone. Hate crime charges that involve sexual orientation or transgender identity kept going up. So being more visible did not stop harm. In some cases, it only made it easier to count the harm.

## 3. Questions about "The topology of race"

**Context**: In this reading, you read a shorter excerpt from a longer review essay by Lee. In this review essay, Lee summarizes Kwame Anthony Appiah's collection of essays, *In My Father's House*, as a means of instigating new lines of inquiry for legal studies.

### 3.1 Race & racism: From conjecture to questions of definition

**Question**: Why does Lee think it important that Appiah changed the standard line of inquiry in legal studies from "whether racism exists" (p. 441) to "what race and racism are"? What "overdetermined" (p. 441) parts or race, i.e., issues about race, does this definitional question bring into focus for deliberation? Be sure to ***define*** and discuss Appiah's position about race as being essential/biological or not and being cultural or not. Indeed, pay close attention to Appiah's definition of culture.

**Response**: Appiah’s shift matters because it changes the question from “does racism exist” to “what are race and racism.” That pushes us to ask how race is made, who uses it, and what it does in real life. It also makes us look at how laws fix these ideas in place. Appiah says races are not real in a biological sense. Those claims are false and harmful. He adds that people often use “race” when they really mean culture. By culture, he means real communities where people share meanings, practices, and ways of understanding themselves. So he suggests we talk in cultural or ethnic terms, not racial ones.

Lee calls race “overdetermined” to say the word carries many layers at the same time. It can point to bodies, history, law, culture, and politics. Asking what race is helps us see each layer and test how race is working in that situation. This matters in court. Judges switch between treating race as skin color and treating it as something built by social rules and discrimination. When they force a simple either or choice, they can block solutions, as happened in Shaw v. Reno.


### 3.2 Race as multiplicitous

**Question**: What does Lee mean by "race is always multiplicitous because social contexts are multiplicitous" (p. 447)? Use the Shaw v. Reno case to help you explain how this challenges both essentialist/biological definitions of race & Appiah's race as synonomous with culture.

**Response**: Lee means race has many meanings because our social worlds have many settings. What race means shifts by context: school, work, voting maps, or courtrooms. In Shaw v. Reno, the Court moved between views. At times it treated race like skin color. At other times it treated race as something made by history and discrimination. That flip shows race is not a single natural fact in the body, but something that changes with place, power, and purpose. This also shows race cannot be reduced to “just culture,” because law and politics actively shape and use race in ways that go beyond shared customs. So, calling race “multiplicitous” pushes back both on biology-only views and on culture-only views. We have to ask what race is doing in each setting, and judge it by its effects there. 

## 4. Questions about a data team's "rhetorical process of data"

### 4.1 What's the "god trick" problem?

**Question**: Briefly describe the "god trick" problem. Also, discuss what connections you recognize between the "god trick" problem and some issues raised in our other readings.

**Response**: The “god trick” is the claim that data or science can see everything from nowhere, as if the work is neutral and outside real life. In reality, data comes from people with goals, tools, and limits, so it always reflects a point of view. This connects to D’Ignazio and Klein, who ask us to examine power, add context, and listen to many voices. It connects to Guyan, who warns that being counted can help or harm depending on who is counting and why. It also connects to Lee, who shows that race shifts meaning across settings, so a view from nowhere cannot explain how race works in law and society. The fix is to show our methods, name our values, and ask who benefits or is put at risk when we use data.

### 4.2 What's stasis and stasis networks?

**Question**: Briefly define stasis and stasis networks. Explain what stasis networks provides qualitative researchers?

**Response**: Stasis means a set of guiding questions people use to think through an issue: What are the facts, what is it, what caused it, how important is it, and what should we do. These questions are often listed in order as fact, cause, definition, quality, and action. 

Stasis networks take those questions and treat them like connected nodes in a real social and technical setting. Instead of a simple step-by-step list, a stasis network maps how people actually move among these questions, how their tools and goals shape choices, and how facts and values mix in practice. It opens the relationships between the stases by combining stasis with sociotechnical network methods, making the “hidden” parts of data work more visible. 

This gives qualitative researchers a practical way to map context. It helps them trace who defined key terms or metrics, how teams judged quality or significance, and how those choices guided what actions were possible.

### 4.3 What's the "DMT cluster"?

**Question**: What's the *DMT cluster*, and how did connect to observations about the data team's judgment about the *quality of metrics*?

**Response**: The DMT cluster is a pattern the team kept falling into. They would define what a metric means, decide how to use it, and talk about the metric itself all at the same time. Lindgren names this DMT from the codes Definitive, Metrics, and Translative (pp. 485 - 486). This cluster links to how they judged metric quality. First, they checked alignment, like making sure “on time” means the same thing in two transit datasets before comparing them. Then, they asked if a metric told a meaningful story, for example choosing a one mile buffer because it made results clearer, and double checking a surprising overtime result before trusting it.
